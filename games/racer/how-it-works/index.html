Neural Racer · How it works

How ten cars learn to drive without a teacher

Nobody writes a driving rule. Nobody shows them a good lap. This is what happens instead, step by step. Every picture on this page is drawn from one real run of the experiment.

5 min readWatch it live

01

The question

Can a car learn to drive if nobody teaches it? In this experiment there is no teacher, no example lap and no rule like "turn when the wall gets close". Each car starts with a brain full of random numbers.

The experiment does only one thing: after every round, it keeps the cars that happened to drive furthest and lets them have children. Everything else on this page follows from that.

02

What a car senses

A car never sees the track. Sixty times a second it gets six numbers: how far the wall is along five feelers (left, slightly left, straight ahead, slightly right and right) and how fast it is going. A feeler reads 1 when the road ahead is clear and drops towards 0 as the wall gets close.

03

What it decides

Those six numbers go into the car's brain, a tiny neural network. The six inputs feed 8 hidden neurons, and those feed 2 outputs: steering (left or right) and throttle (speed up or brake).

Each line in the picture is a weight: a number that says how strongly one neuron pushes the next. A solid line pushes up, a dashed line pushes down. That is all the brain does: six numbers in, two numbers out, again and again.

04

A brain is just 74 numbers

Count every line, plus one extra number per neuron (its bias, a starting nudge), and the whole brain is 74 numbers. Change the numbers and you change how the car drives. Nothing else about it can change.

So a brain can be written as a strip of 74 squares. Think of it as the car's DNA: it is what gets passed on to children.

06

Pick the best

When every car has crashed or stalled, the round ends and the cars are ranked by how far they got. That distance is the only feedback in the whole system. There is no score for style and no hint about why.

The top three become parents. The rest leave no children.

07

Make the next generation

A child takes each of its 74 numbers from one parent or the other, like flipping a coin for every square. That is crossover.

Then a few numbers, about 8 in every 100, get nudged by a small random amount. That is mutation, and it is the only way something new can appear.

The new generation is still ten cars. The best car of the round is copied over unchanged, so a good driver is never lost. Eight are children like the one above. One is a brand-new random brain, to keep fresh ideas coming.

08

Repeat

Drive, rank, breed. Then again. No car ever changes its own brain while it drives. Improvement happens only between generations, through which cars get to have children.

09

Honest notes

  • Luck can win round one. About one run in five, one of the ten random brains finishes in generation 1. That is luck, not learning. The test is whether its children keep finishing.
  • Learned, or memorised? A car might only know this track's corners. On the live page, press New track: if the cars crash, they memorised; if they keep going, they learned to read the walls.
  • Not every run is this tidy. Sometimes the best distance stays flat for several generations until a lucky child breaks through.
10For ML practitioners
Network
Fixed 6–8–2 multilayer perceptron with tanh activations: 6×8 + 8 + 8×2 + 2 = 74 parameters. Inputs are five ray lengths normalised to [0, 1] and speed over top speed. Outputs are steering and throttle in [−1, 1].
Episode
One generation is one episode. A car stops on hitting a wall, after 150 frames without progress, or after 3,600 frames.
Fitness
Furthest progress along the track's centreline, plus a bonus of up to one lap for finishing two laps early. Nothing is rewarded during the episode.
Selection
Truncation: the top 30% (3 of 10) form the parent pool, drawn with a bias towards the top (index = ⌊u² · pool⌋). Elitism keeps the top 10% unchanged; 10% are fresh random genomes.
Variation
Uniform crossover, then per-gene mutation with probability 0.08 (the Mutation slider) and Gaussian noise of σ = 0.4. Initial weights are drawn from N(0, 1).
No gradients
This is a genetic algorithm over the weights of a fixed network (neuroevolution, like NEAT without topology growth). Backpropagation needs a differentiable loss; here the only signal is a distance at the end of an episode, the same setting as reinforcement learning. Evolution strategies have been shown to compete with RL on harder versions of this problem (Salimans et al., 2017).

Your turn
