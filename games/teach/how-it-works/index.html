Teach by Driving · How it works

How a car learns to drive by copying you

You drive. A tiny neural network watches what the car senses and how you steer, then learns to do the same, one small nudge at a time. Then it drives on its own, and crashes exactly where you never showed it what to do.

6 min readTry it live

01

Same brain, a different teacher

In Neural Racer, nobody teaches the cars. Random brains drive, the best ones have children, and driving improves over generations. That is learning from a score.

Here the brain learns from examples instead: you show it what to do, and it learns to copy you. This is how almost every modern AI model is trained, from photo apps to chatbots. The brain is nearly the same: the same six senses, 8 hidden neurons, but only one output, steering. The car holds its own speed, so 65 numbers in all.

02

Every moment is an example

Sixty times a second while you drive, the car writes down two things: the six numbers it sensed, and how you were steering at that moment. That pair is one example: a question and its right answer. One lap makes about 850.

03

A guess, and how wrong it is

Show the untrained brain an example's six numbers and it guesses a steering value. Its weights are random, so the guess is too. The gap between its guess and your answer is its mistake.

Square the gap and halve it, so big mistakes count much more than small ones, then average over every example. That single number is the loss. For the untrained brain it is . Learning means making it smaller.

04

One weight, one hill

Take just one of the 65 weights and slide it, keeping the other 64 still. The loss goes up and down like a hill. Learning is finding the bottom.

The brain cannot see the whole hill. It can only feel the slope right under the ball: that slope is the gradient. So it takes a step downhill, feels the slope again, and steps again. That is gradient descent. The size of each step is the learning rate.

05

All 65 at once

The real thing does this for all 65 weights at the same time: one hill in 65 directions. A method called backpropagation works out the slope for every weight in one go, by tracing each mistake backwards through the network. Every pass over the examples moves every weight a little downhill.

06

Let it drive

Now the brain steers on its own. The loss is low, so it should drive like the teacher.

It drives most of the way like the teacher, then drifts a little wide, and a little more, and hits the wall. Look at the close-up: the brain ended up somewhere the teacher never drove. None of its examples were from there, so it had never learned what to do.

This is called distribution shift: a model meets situations unlike its training data. A small mistake leads to an unfamiliar place, where it makes a bigger mistake. It is one of the main reasons AI that does well in testing can fail in the real world.

07

Take the wheel

The fix is to show it exactly those moments. Let the brain drive again, and grab the wheel whenever it gets into trouble. Every moment you steer becomes a new example, from the very places it gets lost. Then train again on everything.

This idea has a name in robotics research: DAgger, short for Dataset Aggregation. Collect data where the learner actually goes, not just where the expert goes.

09

Honest notes

  • Low loss is not the same as good driving. The loss only measures how well it copies its examples. The brain that crashed in Fig. 6 had a loss; the one that finished in Fig. 7 had . Its corrections were harder examples, and they were the ones that mattered.
  • It copies your habits, good and bad. Hug the wall and it hugs the wall. Weave and it weaves. It can never be a better driver than its examples.
  • It never reaches zero. You never steer exactly the same way twice in the same spot, so there is no single right answer to copy perfectly.
  • The teacher on this page is an autopilot, so the figures are the same on every visit. On the live page the teacher is you.
10For ML practitioners
Task
Behaviour cloning: supervised regression from a 6-number observation (five ray lengths in [0, 1], speed over top speed) to one steering value in [−1, 1]. Throttle is a fixed rule that holds a cruising speed of 1.5 (a third of the racer's top speed), and full steering is 60% of the racer's turn rate, for the human and the network alike, and the road is 85 wide (the racer's is 54) with bends at 70% strength, so a person can drive it with two buttons. One teaching lap; the network is tested over two.
Network
6–8–1 multilayer perceptron, tanh on every layer: 6×8 + 8 + 8×1 + 1 = 65 parameters, initial weights drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.5.
Loss
Half the mean squared error. Labels are the human's steering after a first-order smoothing (each frame moves 25% towards the key held), so they are continuous.
Optimiser
Plain mini-batch stochastic gradient descent: batches of 32, shuffled every epoch, gradients by backpropagation. 40 epochs at first, 30 per retrain, learning rate 0.05 by default.
Corrections
The human can override the policy at any time; only the overridden frames are labelled and added (closer to HG-DAgger, Kelly et al. 2019, than to the original DAgger, Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011, where the expert labels every visited state). The figures use a scripted expert that takes over for 20 frames whenever any ray is under 12% of its range.
Versus Racer
Neural Racer sets similar weights with a genetic algorithm and only a distance score; this game uses labelled targets and exact gradients. Same observation space, different learning signal.

11

Your turn

On the live page you are the teacher. Three things to try:

  1. Drive perfectly down the middle. Then watch where it crashes. Perfect examples teach it nothing about recovering.
  2. Learning rate 3. Once it has learned, move the slider to 3: it learns your same lap again from scratch. Watch the loss chart.
  3. A track it never saw. Once it drives your track, it moves to a new one by itself. Did it learn to drive, or only your track? New track gives it more.
