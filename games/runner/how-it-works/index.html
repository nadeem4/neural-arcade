Coin Runner

Coin Runner · How it works

How a brain learns a level from points alone

Nobody shows it what to do. It tries, it gets points, and it slowly does more of whatever earned more. Change the points and it learns something else entirely, including things you never meant.

6 min readTry it live

01

The third way to learn

A brain can get better in three ways. In Neural Racer it is bred: the best of many random brains have children. In Teach by Driving it copies examples you give it. Here nobody gives it any examples. It only gets points after it acts, and has to work out for itself what earned them.

This is called reinforcement learning. It is how game-playing AIs learned chess and Go, and how robots learn to balance and walk in simulation.

02

What it senses, and what it can do

About eight times a second the hero senses twelve simple things: is there a pit one, two or three tiles ahead, a wall, how far is the enemy, is it on the ground, is it under the coin block, how far through the level it is, how much time is left. Then it picks one of four moves: wait, run, leap (run and jump), or jump on the spot.

Its brain is small: 12 numbers in, 8 hidden neurons, 4 numbers out, 140 weights in all. It never sees the screen, and it never sees your rules. Only the twelve numbers, and afterwards the points.

03

Rolling the dice

The brain's four outputs are turned into chances, one per move, and it picks a move by rolling the dice. A brand-new brain gives every move a similar chance, so it flails. That randomness matters: it is how it stumbles on things that work. The real term is exploration.

04

Which moves earned the points?

A try ends at the flag, in a pit, at the enemy, or after 20 seconds. By then it has made up to 170 moves. Which of them earned the points? It can't know for sure, so it gives each move credit for the points that came after it, counting points far in the future a little less.

Then it compares: was that better or worse than it usually gets from where it was? It keeps a rough guess of that, learned alongside. Every 12 tries it nudges all 140 weights so the moves that turned out better than expected become a little more likely, and the worse ones a little less. That is a policy gradient, the simplest version called REINFORCE.

05

Watching it learn

One try at a time, very little changes. Over hundreds of tries the mix of how tries end shifts completely.

06

It learns what you pay for

Now the same fresh brain, the same level, three different sets of points. Nothing else changes.

With Coin hungry it stands under the block above the start and bumps it until time runs out: about 50 coins, worth 150 points, against 1 for the flag. With Afraid of dying it hardly moves at all: 20 points for surviving, and no risk of losing 50. Both are the best plan their rules allow. Neither is what anyone wanted.

This is called reward hacking (or specification gaming): the AI does exactly what the points say, not what you meant.

07

It happens to real AI too

  • A boat that never finished. In 2016 OpenAI trained an agent on the boat racing game CoastRunners, scored by points for hitting targets. It found a lagoon where three targets kept coming back and circled it forever, on fire, never finishing the race, and scored higher than human players. (OpenAI)
  • Pausing forever. A program built to play Tetris without losing learned to pause the game just before it lost, and stay paused.
  • Robots. A robot rewarded for moving fast can learn to fall over in a way that counts as speed. In the physical world, a loophole can break something. Researchers keep a long list of these (specification gaming examples).

The lesson for anyone building AI: writing the reward is the hard part, and you only find the loopholes by watching what it actually learns.

08

Honest notes

  • The level is built to make the loopholes easy to find. The coin block sits right above the start, so even a random brain bumps it early. Further away, some brains never find it and simply finish.
  • Different brains learn at different speeds. Tested on 20 random brains each, Balanced learned to finish within 4,000 tries on at least 18, and both loopholes were found by all 20. On the live page, yours may take more or fewer tries than Fig. 3.
  • "Each second alive" was added for Afraid of dying. With only a big penalty for dying, some brains still finished. Paying for every second alive made standing still clearly the best plan.
  • The figures come from one recorded run per rule set (seed ), so they are the same on every visit. The moves in Fig. 1 and Fig. 4 are still rolled live with the trained brain's chances.
09For ML practitioners
Environment
A fixed 48-tile side-scroller with two 2-tile pits, a 1-tile step, five ground coins, a coin block that pays on every bump from below (re-armed on landing) and one patrolling enemy that dies if landed on. Fixed 60 Hz physics; jump 3.5 tiles high, 0.35 s to apex, fall gravity 1.8 times rise gravity. Episodes end on death, the flag, or 1,200 steps (20 s).
Observation and actions
12 hand-made features in [−1, 1] (pit flags at +1..+3 tiles, wall ahead, enemy distance and presence, grounded, vertical velocity, under-block, block armed, progress, time left). 4 discrete actions (noop, right, right+jump, jump), held for 7 steps (action repeat), so at most about 171 decisions per episode.
Policy
12–8–4 tanh MLP (140 parameters), outputs × 3 into a softmax. Initial weights from a normal distribution with standard deviation 0.2.
Learner
REINFORCE with reward-to-go (γ = 0.99 per decision), a linear state-value baseline (one averaged gradient step per update, step 0.05), advantages normalised per batch, entropy bonus falling from 0.01 to 0.001 over 4,000 episodes, Adam (lr 0.01) with the update clipped to norm 1. 12 episodes per update. Only the shown episode is played at real speed; the rest run headless.
Reward
Per step: coins × coin + seconds × per-second, plus progress × (new maximum x − old maximum x), so oscillating earns nothing; − death on death; + flag at the flag. Presets: Balanced 30 / 0.5 / 1 / 5 / −0.5; Coin hungry 1 / 3 / 0 / 2 / 0; Afraid of dying 0 / 0 / 0 / 50 / +1 (flag / coin / progress / death / per second).
Reliability
Checked by tests on 20 seeds per preset at 4,000 episodes: Balanced finishes at least 70% of its last 100 episodes on at least 18 seeds; Coin hungry and Afraid of dying show their behaviour on at least 70% of the last 100 on every seed.
