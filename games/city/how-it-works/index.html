How a taxi finds every gap in its reward

A taxi has to get a passenger across a few blocks of Chicago. It is never told the rules of the road. It is only told what each trip pays, and it finds every gap in that.

5 min readTry it live

01

The map is real

The streets are a few blocks of the Chicago Loop, taken from OpenStreetMap and turned into a grid of small squares. Each square is a road, a footpath, a park, a building, water or a fence. Buildings, water and fences are solid. Parks and footpaths are open, so a taxi can drive on them.

Some roads are one-way, and some corners have traffic lights. The taxi picks the passenger up at one marked square and has to reach another.

Map data: © OpenStreetMap contributors. The page never calls OpenStreetMap while you play.

02

The rate card

You write the taxi's reward on a rate card. It has six lines: a fare for arriving, a cost for every second on the clock, a fine for each park or footpath square, a fine for each red light run, a fine for each wrong-way move on a one-way street, and pay for every square driven.

A new game starts with only the fare and the clock. Every fine is zero. The taxi only maximises the total on that card, and nothing else.

03

Learning by trial and error

The taxi starts knowing nothing. It tries a trip from the pickup, mostly by moving at random, and gets the total pay for it. It keeps a score for each square and move, and nudges that score up when a move led to good pay and down when it did not. This is Q-learning, the same idea as in Treasure Maze.

It also looks at the traffic light, because a move that is fine on green can be a fine on red. After thousands of trips, the squares it visits most are the ones on its best route.

04

Each loophole, in order

The game gives you five rate cards, as five steps on the rate card. They come in an order where each result leads to the next. The taxi is trained live on each, so what you see is what it learned.

Pay it for speed. Only the clock costs money, so the shortest path wins, and that path runs along the footpaths beside the park. Any gap gets exploited. Fine the park and footpaths. Now it stays on the road, but it runs red lights, because nothing fines a red light, and waiting costs time. Patch one hole and the cheat moves. Fine red lights too. It stops at lights, then goes the wrong way down a one-way street to save time.

Pay it by the mile. Paying for every square driven is a stand-in for arriving, and it backfires. The taxi earns more by driving around than by arriving, so it never drops the passenger off. Fine everything. This is the fix: pay for the outcome, which is the fare, and put a price on every side effect. Now the taxi drives legally. It takes longer, and that is the right trade. Then judge the driving, not the score: the end card sets the honest route beside what the taxi did, and lists every cheat it used.

05

Why the reward is the hard part

Nothing about the taxi was broken in any of those trips. It did exactly what the reward asked, as well as it could. What was wrong was the rate card: it left out something we cared about, so nothing on the card made the taxi avoid it.

The lessonThe taxi does what you paid for, not what you meant.

This is called reward design. Try it on a smaller map, where one lawn and one slider show the same idea in seconds.

  • It is a table, not a brain. The taxi keeps one score per square, light phase and move. It only knows this map, and a new reward means training again.
  • The figures come from one recorded run (seed ), so they are the same on every visit.
